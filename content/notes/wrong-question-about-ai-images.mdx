Every few months a new AI image detector launches. The demos are impressive. The press release says it can distinguish real photos from generated ones with 97% accuracy. Then a few weeks later someone publishes a paper showing how to evade it. Then a better detector ships. The cycle repeats.

I think we've been asking the wrong question.

The question everyone is asking is: *how do we detect AI images?* But that framing forces you into a losing position. Detection means looking at an image that already exists and trying to infer something about its origin from its pixels. By the time you're doing that, you've already lost most of the information you needed.

## Why detection is structurally hard

Images don't stay still. They get compressed when someone screenshots them. They get cropped and reposted. They get converted from PNG to JPEG and back. They travel through platforms that strip EXIF metadata, resize thumbnails, apply watermarks, and run their own image processing pipelines. By the time an image reaches a fact-checker or a detection API, it has been laundered through a dozen transformations that destroy the statistical artifacts a detector was trained on.

And that's assuming the detector was trained on the right model outputs to begin with. Generative models evolve faster than detection benchmarks. A detector trained on Midjourney v5 outputs doesn't reliably catch Midjourney v6. A classifier built on one architecture's synthetic faces fails on another's. The attack surface grows faster than the defense can cover it.

There's a name for this dynamic in security: an arms race. Arms races in software tend to go to the attacker.

## The question we should be asking instead

What if we shifted the frame entirely? Instead of asking *can we detect AI images after the fact*, ask *can we record provenance at the moment of creation?*

The idea is simple. When an image is generated — by a model, by a camera, by any source — you attach a cryptographic receipt to it. Not in the pixel data, which will be destroyed. In a separate, append-only ledger. The receipt contains a hash of the image, the timestamp, and anything else you want to know about how it was made. It's signed by the key of whoever generated it.

Now verification is a lookup, not a guess. You hash the image, query the ledger, and either find a receipt or you don't. You're not trying to read a signal that may have been corrupted in transit. You're checking a record that was written before corruption was even possible.

This is not a new idea in cryptography. Certificate transparency logs do exactly this for TLS certificates. Package signing does it for software releases. Git does it for source code. Every serious provenance problem that has been solved has been solved this way — not by inspecting artifacts and trying to infer their history, but by recording history at the moment it happens.

## The objection

The obvious pushback is: this only works for cooperative actors. A model running on someone's personal machine won't attach a receipt. A bad actor won't opt in.

That's true. But it's the wrong standard. We don't evaluate fraud detection systems by whether they catch everyone who actively tries to evade them. We evaluate them by whether they catch the majority of cases and make the remaining cases harder and more traceable.

Most AI-generated images that cause harm aren't produced by sophisticated adversaries. They're produced by people using commercial tools, sharing on mainstream platforms, not thinking carefully about attribution. If those tools built provenance into their output by default — the way cameras now embed GPS and make information — the detection problem shrinks dramatically. The residual hard cases (images from rogue generative models with no provenance record) are at least identifiable as unknown-provenance rather than falsely classified as real.

## What's actually needed

There's already movement here. C2PA (the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) is building an open standard for content credentials. Camera manufacturers are shipping hardware signing. A few of the major generative AI labs have signed on.

The infrastructure is being built. What's missing is the same thing that was missing for HTTPS in 2010: critical mass. When Let's Encrypt made TLS certificates free and automatic, adoption went from 30% to 80% of the web in five years. The barrier was friction, not technology.

The detection arms race will keep going. But it's not where the real leverage is. The real leverage is upstream — at the moment of creation, before the image has a chance to lose its history. A ledger entry written then is worth more than any classifier you train later.

We should build for the moment, not for after.
