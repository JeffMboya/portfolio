There is a specific kind of exhaustion that comes from debugging ladder logic in the morning and tracing goroutine leaks in the afternoon.

I spent the first two years of my career at Octavia Carbon with a laptop perpetually open to TIA Portal on one monitor and a terminal on the other. The PLC was a Siemens S7-1200 running the adsorption and regeneration cycles for a direct air capture machine. The terminal was usually connected to a Go service handling sensor data or command dispatch.

At first glance these feel like completely different disciplines. Ladder logic is visual, sequential, deterministic. Go is concurrent, compiled, text-based. One runs on dedicated hardware with microsecond scan cycles; the other runs on a Linux box somewhere and gets scheduled by the OS.

But the longer I worked across both, the more I noticed the same failure modes appearing in different clothes.

## The bug that taught me this

The PLC had a timer block controlling how long the system stayed in the adsorption phase before switching to regeneration. The logic looked correct — the timer would hit its preset, the output coil would energise, and the system would transition. Except sometimes it wouldn't. The machine would stay in adsorption 10-15 seconds longer than it should.

The fix, after two days of staring at rungs, was embarrassingly simple: the timer's input coil was being reset by a separate rung that evaluated slightly out of phase during high-CPU-load scan cycles. The timer was restarting itself intermittently.

A few months later, building a Go service for the same system, I hit a bug where a goroutine handling thermal sensor readings would occasionally stop sending updates. The service looked healthy — no panics, no errors in the log. The goroutine was just silently blocking on a channel write because the reader had fallen behind and the channel buffer was full.

The same bug. Different clothes. In both cases: a timing-dependent interaction between two concurrent things, invisible until load hit a certain threshold, silent failure mode, no error raised.

## What transfers

Debugging embedded systems teaches you to think in scan cycles — everything has a rate, and mismatches between rates cause bugs. Debugging concurrent software teaches you to think in schedules — goroutines, threads, and channels all have their own rates, and the same mismatches appear.

If you've built a PLC program, you already understand why you should never block the main loop. If you understand why you should never block the main loop, you already have the intuition for why goroutine leaks are dangerous.

The vocabulary is different. The mental model is the same: concurrent state, deterministic-ish transitions, timing that matters.

I don't know if this transfers to everyone. But coming from hardware to software, I found it easier to reason about concurrency than I expected — because I'd already spent years reasoning about it in a different language.
