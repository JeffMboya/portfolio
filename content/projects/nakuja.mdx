## Why Rockets

I joined the Nakuja Project because I wanted to work on problems where failure was physical, visible, and expensive.

Rockets are honest systems — bad assumptions explode quickly.

They force clarity. They punish hand-waving. They demand discipline in design, humility in testing, and respect for reality.

I wanted to work on engineering problems where success depended not just on clever ideas, but on whether the system actually worked when ignition happened.

That was what drew me to propulsion.

What kept me there was realising that rockets are never just about rockets.

They depend on teams, test systems, documentation, iteration speed, institutional trust, and whether knowledge survives the people who built the first version.

At Nakuja, the mission is ambitious: build a liquid-propellant rocket capable of placing nanosatellites into low Earth orbit and make Kenya an active contributor to the global space economy.

The work is open source, low-cost, student-operated, and built in partnership with the Kenya Space Agency.

That combination — serious engineering with institutional ambition — is why I stayed.

## Systems I Changed

### Scaling the Team from 6 to 40+ Contributors

One of the first things I noticed was how much progress depended on a very small group of people.

A postgraduate team of six researchers was carrying most of the technical work across propulsion, avionics, structures, and mission operations. The challenge was not a lack of ideas. It was limited execution capacity and too much knowledge concentrated in too few people.

I proposed and led the creation of an internship pipeline that grew Nakuja into a multi-university engineering community of more than 40 active contributors.

That meant designing structured onboarding, technical mentorship, clearer documentation, and stronger collaboration across sub-teams. New contributors needed to become productive quickly, and knowledge needed to remain accessible after people moved on.

The result was faster iteration, stronger institutional continuity, and a team that could move beyond isolated experiments toward repeatable engineering progress.

That growth contributed directly to the successful N-2 launch, long-term programme sustainability, and a strategic collaboration with the Kenya Space Agency.

It taught me that scaling people and scaling hardware follow the same rules: good feedback loops, reliable systems, and clear interfaces.

### Engineering That Survived Peer Review

I never cared much about publishing papers.

What mattered to me was whether our engineering decisions could survive criticism from people who had no reason to be polite.

I co-authored two peer-reviewed research papers based on propulsion systems developed within the Nakuja Project and served as primary author on one.

The work focused on the design, testing, and performance validation of solid rocket motors for student-built sounding rockets:

- *Development of Solid Propellant Motor for Low Altitude Model Rockets* — N-2 motor (35 N average thrust, 34 m apogee)
- *Development of a Solid Propellant Motor for High-Powered Model Rockets* — N-3 motor (151.7 N average thrust, 280 m apogee)

Writing the papers was the easy part.

The difficult part was defending decisions.

Why this grain geometry? Why this propellant composition? Why this nozzle profile? Why should anyone trust the test data?

Both papers were presented at JKUAT-COETEC 2024 and both received Best Presentation awards — breaking a decade-long precedent by putting students at the centre of technical proceedings instead of the margins.

That recognition mattered because it showed the work held up under scrutiny.

Research matters when assumptions survive attack.

## Key Engineering Work

Most of my work sat between analysis and execution — improving how we designed, tested, and learned from solid rocket motors.

I cared about reducing the distance between an idea and knowing whether it was wrong.

That meant better models, stronger test systems, and faster feedback loops.

### +5% Motor Efficiency

I performed numerical performance analysis of solid rocket motors using MATLAB simulations and OpenMotor, validated against static-fire test data.

The goal was prediction, not prettier graphs.

I developed a combustion model that captured burn behaviour accurately enough to support parameter optimisation and improve motor efficiency by 5%.

In propulsion, small efficiency gains carry real consequences. A few percentage points can decide whether a design is ready for flight or whether the motor architecture needs to be reconsidered entirely.

### +10% Testing Efficiency / −15% Turnaround Time

I helped design and establish a new solid rocket engine test facility, including a custom data acquisition system with real-time performance monitoring.

Testing speed shapes engineering speed. Weak test infrastructure makes every decision slower and more expensive.

The new setup reduced post-test processing overhead significantly and improved turnaround time by 15%, allowing faster design iteration cycles.

A strong test stand often creates more progress than a clever design because it shortens the path between failure and learning.

### −50% Data Analysis Time

Too much engineering time was being lost waiting for raw sensor output to become useful information.

I worked with a teammate to build a Python automation pipeline for test-stand data processing, cutting the analysis cycle in half.

That reduced the time from raw data to actionable insight by 50% and allowed the team to run more test iterations per sprint.

Engineering speed is often limited by latency, not intelligence.

### +15% Motor Thrust

Most optimisation work starts after something underperforms.

I conducted post-test analysis of engine data and identified propellant mixture composition and grain geometry as the highest-leverage variables affecting thrust performance.

Those recommendations were incorporated into later motor revisions and produced a measurable 15% increase in thrust output.

Performance improvements usually come from asking better questions, not from working longer hours.

### Zero Safety Incidents

Propulsion teaches respect.

I supported the fabrication and assembly of solid rocket motor components — including propellant grain preparation, motor casing assembly, and nozzle integration — under strict quality and safety protocols.

Across manufacturing, testing, and launch operations, the propulsion programme maintained a clean safety record while successfully completing its first integrated propulsion system test launch.

In rocketry, safety is inseparable from engineering quality.

## Propulsion Development

Each rocket in the Nakuja series uses a custom-designed solid propellant motor built entirely by the team.

The propellant uses a potassium nitrate / sucrose-dextrose mixture chosen for availability, cost, and predictable burn characteristics. The nozzle geometry is convergent-divergent, machined in-house from mild steel.

Every design is validated through static-fire testing on a purpose-built thrust stand instrumented with load cells and a custom data acquisition system that logs thrust, chamber pressure, and burn time.

The work is deliberately low-cost and practical: build, test, fail, improve, repeat.

## Broglio, KSA, and Seeing the Bigger System

Working with the Kenya Space Agency changed how I thought about the project.

KSA facilitated launch clearances and hosted our team at the Broglio Space Center in Malindi — Kenya's historic satellite tracking facility.

Seeing the ground station infrastructure, satellite dish arrays, and orbital operations firsthand made something clear: rockets are only one layer of the system.

Launch vehicles, mission operations, tracking infrastructure, regulatory approvals, and institutional partnerships all determine whether space engineering works in practice.

That experience changed how I think about technical work.

The hardest engineering problems are rarely isolated technical problems. They are system problems.

That lesson shaped how I approach both rockets and teams.

## How I Think

Engineering leverage comes from three things.

**Better models.** If you can predict behaviour before expensive failure, you move faster. That applies to combustion chambers and to organisations.

**Better systems.** A strong test stand beats heroic debugging. Reliable feedback loops outperform isolated brilliance.

**Better people.** The best technical work compounds when knowledge survives individuals. Good engineering should outlast the engineer.

That is why I care equally about propulsion models, test infrastructure, and internship pipelines.

They all solve the same problem: reducing friction between intention and execution.
