## Why Nakuja

I joined the Nakuja Project because I wanted to work on engineering problems where failure was physical, visible, and expensive.

Rockets are honest systems. Bad assumptions fail quickly.

They force clarity, punish hand-waving, and demand discipline in design, testing, and decision-making. Success depends less on clever ideas and more on whether the system actually works when ignition happens.

That was what drew me to propulsion.

What kept me there was realizing that rockets are never just about rockets. They depend on teams, test systems, documentation, iteration speed, institutional trust, and whether knowledge survives the people who built the first version.

Nakuja's mission is ambitious: build a liquid-propellant rocket capable of placing nanosatellites into low Earth orbit and make Kenya an active contributor to the global space economy.

The work is open source, low-cost, student-operated, and built in partnership with the Kenya Space Agency.

That combination — serious engineering with institutional ambition — is why I stayed.

## Systems I Changed

### Scaling the Team from 6 to 40+ Contributors

One of the first things I noticed at the Nakuja Project was how much progress depended on a very small group of people.

An undergraduate team of six researchers was carrying most of the technical work across propulsion, avionics, structures, and mission operations. The challenge was not a shortage of ideas, but limited execution capacity and too much critical knowledge concentrated in too few people.

I proposed and led the creation of an internship pipeline that grew Nakuja into a multi-university engineering community of more than 40 active contributors.

This involved building structured onboarding, technical mentorship, clearer documentation, and stronger collaboration across sub-teams. New contributors needed enough context to become productive quickly, and knowledge needed to remain accessible after people moved on.

The result was faster iteration, stronger institutional continuity, and a team that could move beyond isolated experiments toward repeatable engineering progress.

That growth contributed directly to the successful N-2 launch, long-term program sustainability, and stronger collaboration with the Kenya Space Agency.

It also changed how I think about engineering leadership. Scaling people and scaling hardware both depend on good feedback loops, reliable systems, and clear interfaces.

### Engineering That Survived Peer Review

I co-authored two peer-reviewed research papers based on propulsion systems developed within the Nakuja Project and served as primary author on one.

The work focused on the design, testing, and validation of solid rocket motors for student-built sounding rockets:

* **Development of Solid Propellant Motor for Low Altitude Model Rockets** — N-2 motor (35 N average thrust, 500 m target altitude)
* **Development of a Solid Propellant Motor for High-Powered Model Rockets** — N-3 motor (151.7 N average thrust, ~1.6 km simulated apogee)

The value of the work was not in writing the papers, but in being able to defend the engineering decisions behind them.

Every choice had to be justified: grain geometry, propellant composition, nozzle profile, burn behavior, and whether the test data was reliable enough to support design conclusions.

Both papers were presented at the Sustainable Research and Innovation Conference, and both received Best Presentation awards. It was the first time in more than a decade that student-led work received that level of recognition in the proceedings.

That mattered because it showed the propulsion work held up under technical scrutiny.

## Engineering Impact

Most of my work sat between analysis and execution — improving how we designed, tested, and learned from solid rocket motors.

I cared about reducing the distance between an idea and knowing whether it was wrong.

That meant better models, stronger test systems, and faster feedback loops.

### +5% Motor Efficiency

I performed numerical performance analysis of solid rocket motors using MATLAB simulations and OpenMotor, validated against static-fire test data.

Static-fire validated burn-rate optimization improved motor efficiency by 5% over the previous baseline motor design.

Small efficiency gains in propulsion carry real consequences. A few percentage points can decide whether a design is ready for flight or whether the motor architecture needs to be reconsidered entirely.

### +10% Testing Efficiency / −15% Turnaround Time

I helped design and establish a new solid rocket engine test facility, including a custom data acquisition system with real-time performance monitoring.

Weak test infrastructure makes every engineering decision slower and more expensive.

The new setup reduced post-test processing overhead and improved turnaround time by 15%, allowing faster design iteration cycles and stronger test reliability.

### −50% Data Analysis Time

I worked with a teammate to build a Python automation pipeline for processing test-stand data from static-fire tests.

Before that, moving from raw sensor output to usable engineering insight took too long and slowed down iteration across the propulsion team. Test results often required manual cleanup, repeated calculations, and delayed post-test analysis.

We built a workflow that automated data cleaning, processing, and performance interpretation, making it easier to move from thrust data and burn measurements to actual design decisions.

This reduced analysis time by 50% and allowed the team to run more test iterations within each sprint.

Faster engineering often comes from reducing delay between testing and learning, not from working longer hours.

### +15% Motor Thrust

Post-test analysis showed that propellant mixture composition and grain geometry were the highest-leverage variables affecting thrust performance.

Those recommendations were incorporated into later motor revisions and produced a measurable 15% increase in thrust output.

Performance improvements usually come from asking better questions, not from working longer hours.

### Propulsion Manufacturing and Test Discipline

I supported the fabrication and assembly of solid rocket motor components, including propellant grain preparation, motor casing assembly, and nozzle integration, under strict quality and safety procedures.

Across manufacturing, testing, and launch operations, the propulsion program maintained a clean safety record while successfully completing its first integrated propulsion system test launch.

In rocketry, safety is inseparable from engineering quality.

## Propulsion Development

Each rocket in the Nakuja series uses a custom-designed solid propellant motor built entirely by the team.

The propellant uses a potassium nitrate / sucrose-dextrose mixture chosen for availability, cost, and predictable burn characteristics. The nozzle geometry is convergent-divergent and machined in-house from mild steel.

Every design is validated through static-fire testing on a purpose-built thrust stand instrumented with load cells and a custom data acquisition system logging thrust, chamber pressure, and burn time.

The work is deliberately low-cost and practical: build, test, fail, improve, repeat.

## Broglio, KSA, and Systems Thinking

Working with the Kenya Space Agency changed how I thought about the project.

KSA facilitated launch clearances and hosted our team at the Broglio Space Center — Kenya's historic satellite tracking facility.

Seeing the ground station infrastructure, satellite dish arrays, and orbital operations firsthand made something clear: rockets are only one layer of the system.

Launch vehicles, mission operations, tracking infrastructure, regulatory approvals, and institutional partnerships all determine whether space engineering works in practice.

The hardest engineering problems are rarely isolated technical problems. They are system problems.

That lesson shaped how I approach both rockets and teams.

## How I Think

Engineering leverage comes from three things.

**Better models** — If you can predict behavior before expensive failure, you move faster.

**Better systems** — Strong test infrastructure beats heroic debugging.

**Better people** — The best technical work compounds when knowledge survives individuals.

That is why I care equally about propulsion models, test infrastructure, and internship pipelines.

They all solve the same problem: reducing friction between intention and execution.
