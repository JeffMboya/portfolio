I joined Octavia Carbon as employee #24 when the company had already built its first DAC machine—but it did not work.

There was no reliable regeneration cycle, controls were unstable, instrumentation was unreliable, and the system could not produce commercial-grade CO₂ output. Operation was manual or semi-automatic, data quality was poor, and regeneration times were too long to make the system economically viable.

I became the first engineer owning electrical, instrumentation, and controls, and helped turn that failed prototype into the Global South's first fully deployed commercial Direct Air Capture system operating at 99.9% purity.

I broke a two-year technical deadlock, led the controller redesign that reduced CAPEX by 63%, and helped validate the scale-up path from TRL6 pilot systems (1 kg/day and 2 ton/year) to a 60 ton/year commercial field unit.

## Why This Work Mattered

We were not improving an existing industry.

We were trying to prove one could exist.

Octavia is building the first Direct Air Capture system in the Global South. There was no blueprint for what we were trying to do in Kenya.

If we succeeded, we proved that the Global South could lead carbon removal infrastructure instead of importing it. If we failed, it could discourage climate investment across the continent for years.

At the same time, DAC only works if costs fall below the industry target of $100 per ton.

Every engineering decision—controls, regeneration speed, purity, CAPEX, and energy recovery—was a direct bet on whether that would ever be possible.

---

## Engineering Impact

Most of my work sat between technical correctness and commercial viability—making sure the machine captured CO₂ efficiently enough, cheaply enough, and reliably enough to justify deployment.

That meant solving both physics and economics at the same time.

---

### 63% CAPEX Reduction — Rebuilding the Control Stack

My proudest engineering decision was replacing the off-the-shelf industrial controller architecture with a custom proprietary control stack built around in-house hardware and firmware.

The original off-the-shelf setup was the right prototype choice for an early machine, where reliability and mature tooling mattered more than cost or flexibility. But as the system moved toward commercial readiness, it became economically impossible to scale.

The platform could not support the sensor density required for R&D units without expensive expansion modules, limited customization, and poor long-term unit economics.

I initiated and led the transition.

I owned:

* PCB design for sensor conditioning and actuator interfacing
* firmware architecture for distributed control
* validation and deployment across R&D machines
* the full build-vs-buy analysis
* rollout of the proprietary controller stack across pilot systems

This decision delivered the full 63% CAPEX reduction in control architecture costs while improving flexibility, OTA updates, custom I/O density, and long-term deployment economics.

It removed one of the biggest commercialization barriers in the company.

---

### Breaking a 2-Year Technical Deadlock

For nearly two years, capture and regeneration inefficiency prevented the system from reaching commercial-grade output.

The machine could run, but it could not produce stable CO₂ output pure enough for deployment.

I led the work that broke that deadlock.

This included:

* replacing thermocouples with RTDs to eliminate temperature-control inaccuracies
* writing automated control programs to remove operator-driven process variability
* redesigning seal interfaces and vacuum draw paths to ensure complete purge before regeneration
* improving regeneration consistency through better thermal control and sequencing

The result was a stable 24/5 industrial operation achieving 99.9% product purity every cycle—directly enabling the first fully deployed commercial DAC system in the Global South.

This was the moment the company moved from a lab experiment to an industrial system.

---

### 83% Faster Regeneration / 3.6× CO₂ Throughput

The binding constraint on unit economics was regeneration cycle time.

How long it took to regenerate the adsorbent determined throughput, energy consumption, and ultimately cost per ton of CO₂ removed.

I diagnosed regeneration as the critical bottleneck and led 3 cross-functional teams to redesign the system around:

* heat transfer improvements
* airflow optimization
* regeneration sequence redesign
* sorbent type selection and packing
* thermal recovery improvements

The outcome was an 83% reduction in regeneration cycle time and a 3.6× increase in daily CO₂ throughput.

This materially moved the system toward the sub-$100/ton commercial target.

---

### 0% Downtime Across 400+ Test Cycles

I led a 5-person team operating the 2 ton/year TRL6 pilot plant and maintained 0% downtime across more than 400 test cycles.

This was made possible by stronger controls, maintenance discipline, calibration schedules, fault detection, and faster operational debugging.

Without repeatability, scale is only a theory.

I co-developed the technical R&D roadmap and due diligence framework that helped secure $3.1M in Series A funding.

I contributed through:

## Research and Validation

I co-authored:

**Simulation and Airflow Experimentation of a Multi-Layer Adsorbent Chamber for Enhanced Direct Air Capture Efficiency**

Conventional axial-flow DAC systems force air directly through packed adsorbent beds—high resistance, high fan energy, and poor economics.

We designed a multi-layer chamber that routed airflow laterally across thinner adsorbent sections in parallel, reducing pressure drop and fan load.

The design was simulated in Ansys and validated experimentally against prototype airflow measurements.

The work was presented at the Sustainable Research and Innovation Conference (2025).

Research mattered because it improved unit economics, not because it became a paper.

## Building the Team

As the system scaled, so did the team.

I led the conversion of 9 interns into full-time engineering contributors—building the execution capacity required to move from prototype experimentation to repeatable industrial operation.

The challenge was not hiring.

It was making people productive inside a system still being built.

That meant clearer ownership, stronger operational discipline, and faster collaboration across mechanical, chemical, and controls teams.

## How I Think

Climate hardware is unforgiving.

Reliable systems come from three things:

**Correct physics** — the process must work thermodynamically before it works financially.

**Control over the stack** — the economics improve when you own the system instead of renting critical decisions from suppliers.

**Operational discipline** — a machine is only real when it can run repeatedly without heroics.

That is why I care equally about controls, process design, and team structure.

They all solve the same problem:

turning technical possibility into commercial reality.
